# Incident report — "random buys, said on-chain but wasn't, emptied my wallet"

Date: 2026-08-31 · Branch: `claude/bot-trading-audit-fix-oevctr` off `master` @ `820132a` (v3.0.6)
Audit: 14 parallel lanes, **117 findings** (32 critical, 53 high, 28 medium, 4 low), then a 7-lens adversarial review of the fix itself.

---

## The one-paragraph version

Three separate defects produced the three symptoms, and none of them was a mystery once the code was read. **A buy that never confirmed was recorded as an open position with invented token quantities, and the UI labelled it "ON-CHAIN"** — that text was rendered by the exact code path where confirmation had *failed*. **A leader's airdrop or bag-transfer was classified as a purchase** whenever a DEX program merely appeared among the transaction's accounts, so the bot bought tokens the leader had not bought. And **split sizing handed a single order the entire remaining wallet** whenever the position book was full, which is the normal steady state of a working bot — while *nothing at all* bounded copy-trade spending, because every circuit breaker in the codebase was wired into the sniper's entry gate and the copy trader reaches the signer through a different door. All three are fixed, with tests that reproduce the old behaviour first. Underneath them sat a fourth problem worth naming on its own: the suite reported "436 passed" through all of it, because no test had ever executed the real-mode copy buy path.

---

## The three symptoms, and what actually caused each

### S2 — "saying its on chain but its not"

`executeRealMainnetTrade` polled for 30 seconds and collapsed everything it could not resolve into one word, `'timeout'`. For a **buy** it then did this:

```js
if (confirmed === 'timeout') {
  // "funds may have moved"
  return { txid, fill: null };
}
```

A non-null return means success to every caller. So `copyTraderService.onLeaderBuy` invented the quantities — `tokensBought = copySol / leaderPriceSol`, `solSpent = copySol` — and opened a position; `sniperEngine.executeBuy` fell through with its pre-trade estimates (or a literal `1_000_000` tokens). The badge rendered **both** verified and unverified fills as "ON-CHAIN", and the unverified tooltip read *"Submitted and confirmed on-chain"*.

Three things made `'timeout'` far more common than it looks:

1. `getSignatureStatus` was called with no options, so `searchTransactionHistory` defaulted to **false**. A signature aged out of the node's recent-status cache reads as `null` forever — indistinguishable from "not landed".
2. The poll loop swallowed every RPC error in a bare `catch {}`. A rate-limited key means *every* poll throws for the full 30s, so **every trade in that window became a phantom position**.
3. 30s is shorter than a blockhash lifetime. The bot gave up while the transaction could still land.

And nothing ever reconciled the book against the wallet: `syncPositionsWithOnChainBalances` existed but was reachable only from a button, so a phantom was permanent — it held a position slot and its exit-gas reserve for the rest of the session and survived restarts.

**Fixed.** A new settlement module asks `getSignatureStatuses` *with* history search, separates "the chain says no" from "the RPC would not talk to us", and **proves** the negative case via `isBlockhashValid` (two consecutive observations, so a last-slot landing is not called dead). `expired` opens nothing. `unknown` reads the **wallet's real balance** before recording anything, and if that is also unreadable, nothing is opened and the mint is queued for reconciliation with a loud untracked-bag warning. No real position, in either engine, is ever opened from an invented quantity. The book is reconciled against the chain automatically every 90s. The badge no longer says "ON-CHAIN" for anything unproven.

### S1 — "random buys"

`classifyFlow` returned `'trade'` for any token inflow when either of two conditions held, and both are much weaker than they look:

```js
if (p.isTokenSwap || p.venueKnown) return 'trade';
```

`venueKnown` comes from `detectVenue`, which scans a transaction's **account keys** — it answers "was a DEX program mentioned anywhere in this transaction", not "did the leader swap". A bundle, a program-routed airdrop, a multi-recipient distribution, another party's swap the leader was merely referenced by: all satisfied it. `isTokenSwap` is computed upstream as "some mint went up and some mint went down", which a bag transferred *out* alongside an airdrop landing satisfies exactly.

Either produced a BUY signal carrying `solAmount: 0`, and nothing downstream refused it: `minLeaderBuySol` defaults to 0, and split sizing never consults the leader's size at all. The bot bought, at a full slice, a token the leader had not bought.

**Fixed.** A buy now requires that the leader actually gave something up — SOL above a floor, or a token-for-token swap that at least executed on a venue we recognise. The sell side is deliberately unchanged: acting on a false sell exits a bag we already hold, which is far cheaper than buying a token nobody chose. `addWallet` also refuses the bot's own signer, which was an unbounded self-feeding loop. The fast lane, which acts on unconfirmed `'processed'` logs by design, now verifies its own premise after the fact and names any copy made on a leader transaction that did not survive.

### S3 — "it emptied my wallet"

Two mechanisms, one on top of the other.

**The sizing cliff.** `splitStakeSol` computed its divisor as `Math.max(1, slots - openPositionCount)`, and the comment called the floor "only a guard" on the reasoning that the book cap is enforced separately. It is *not* enforced separately for a repeat buy: `onLeaderBuy` skips the `maxOpenPositions` check entirely when a position for the mint already exists, because a repeat buy merges into it. So with the book full — `open == slots`, the normal steady state of a working bot — a repeat buy arrived with the subtraction giving 0, the floor turned it into 1, and `splitWalletIntoSlots` divided the deployable balance by **one**. One slot became the whole wallet, and `blockRepeatBuys` defaults to `false`, so there was nothing upstream to stop it repeating.

**No ceiling at all.** `guardrails.ts` has an entry-rate limiter, a consecutive-transaction-failure breaker and a feed-freshness gate. Every one of them was evaluated in exactly one place: the sniper's own entry gate. The copy trader calls `executeExternalTrade`, which drops straight into `executeRealMainnetTrade` — below all of them. This was not an oversight that went unnoticed; it was written down, in `noteTradeFailure`:

> *"Copy-trade orders share the signer but not the breaker. […] The copy trader runs its own bounded retries instead."*

It had none. So the copy path could buy on every leader signal (`perWalletCooldownSec` defaults to 0), re-buy the same mint without limit, and burn a base fee plus a priority fee on every buy that **failed** on-chain — forever, because a failed buy opens no position and therefore never consumes a slot either. The loss-based kill switch could not help: it reads only the sniper's trade history and is gated on the sniper being active, so someone running copy trading with the sniper stopped had no hourly cap, no daily cap and no consecutive-loss cap of any kind.

**Fixed.** The divisor floor is gone and a repeat buy is sized at one full-book slice; new entries keep the self-correcting free-slot divisor, so slices do not decay. And a new **spend governor** enforces a wallet reserve floor, per-mint SOL and count ceilings, rolling-hour and per-session SOL ceilings, a session realized-loss cap, a concurrency ceiling, a balance-staleness refusal and a consecutive-failure latch — **inside `executeRealMainnetTrade`**, the one function every real order passes through, so it cannot be walked around. Buys only: a breaker that could block an exit would strand bags.

## The two things that made it worse

**All-in sizing turned itself on.** `App.tsx` called `fetchFlags()` *after* the effect's cleanup `return`, so it was unreachable — the UI never once read the server's real feature flags and permanently displayed the hardcoded initial state, in which `allInSizing` was `true`. `handleSaveConfig` then POSTed that frozen `true` back with every settings save. **Saving a Helius key turned on 100%-of-wallet sizing.** Separately, `DEFAULTS.allInSizing` was `true` — the one entry in a table whose own comment calls it "legacy behaviour, everything off" that was actually on — and the project's documented launcher, `run bot real.cmd`, ends in `node dist\server.js` with no `SNIPER_PACKAGED`, so it landed on that table with the kill switch, honeypot check, dev-sell stop and economics gate all off.

**The forensic log went nowhere.** `bot.log` re-derived its own base directory and missed the `SNIPER_DATA_DIR` branch, so under the installed desktop app it resolved to `process.cwd()` — `C:\Windows\System32` from a Start-menu shortcut, or the read-only install directory. Every append failed, and failures there are swallowed by design. The only durable record of what the bot did did not exist for exactly the people running the installer. That is why an incident can be reported with no way to tell a phantom position from a real one.

Both fixed. The flags fetch is reachable, the flag is no longer smuggled through the config save, `allInSizing` is off in both tables, the launcher sets `SNIPER_PACKAGED=1`, and `bot.log` goes through the shared path helper.

## Why the tests said everything was fine

- **No test ever executed the real-mode copy buy.** `copySellDrill.ts` is the only file that drives the copy trader end to end, and it sets `tradingMode: 'paper'`. The branch that reserves SOL, calls the engine and decides whether a position exists had **zero** offline coverage, so every regression in it was discovered with real money.
- **The release gate ran a subset.** `release.yml` ran `npm test`, which is `run.ts` alone — the copy drills, the guardrail proofs and the auto-update proofs never ran at release.
- **Two tests asserted the bug.** One pinned `DEFAULTS.allInSizing === true`; another locked in the classifier rule that made an airdrop a purchase.

`copyRealBuyDrill.ts` now drives the real path with the engine stubbed at its single seam (21 checks, no chain access): a landed buy with a readable fill, a buy that never lands, a landed buy whose fill is unreadable, one with no readable size at all, a balance-derived DCA top-up, a latched governor, STOP arriving while a buy is queued, and split sizing on a full book. `incidentProofs.ts` adds 51 proofs that reproduce each old behaviour before asserting the new one. The release gate runs `npm run test:all` plus an explicit typecheck. The two bug-pinning tests now assert the fix.

## Also fixed (selected)

**Money.** A double-sell: `resolveTimedOutSell` re-implemented the same confirmation bug and reported `'expired'` — a licence to resubmit — when the RPC simply would not answer, so a percentage sell executed twice. Exits booked from the fraction *requested* rather than what moved, and invented proceeds from a stale price when the fill was unreadable. A failed automatic exit never checked the chain, so a bag already gone re-ran the six-attempt sell loop and its fees on every subsequent leader signal. A "bad fill" abort compared the real fill against a **fabricated** decision price and force-liquidated good entries. `clampPriorityFeeSol` applied the configured floor *after* the ceiling, so `priorityFeeSol` overrode `maxPriorityFeeSol`.

**State.** `.copy-trader.json` was written with a bare `writeFileSync`; a crash mid-write left it truncated, the next boot's parse threw inside a catch, and the service started on `DEFAULT_CONFIG` — every open position, every tracked leader and every hardened setting silently replaced by more permissive defaults. It also recorded no wallet address, so positions were reconciled against whatever key was linked later. Restored sniper positions were printed to a console nobody sees and then discarded, so every restart permanently orphaned open bags. The position store wrote to the wrong directory under the packaged app. Two instances could arm real copy trading on the same wallet, because only the sniper took the machine-wide lock — and stopping the sniper *released* it while copy trading was still live.

**Silence.** The sniper's entire exit engine — every take-profit rung, every stop, the liquidation path — was wrapped in `catch (err) { /* ignore */ }`. One throw disabled every automatic exit for that position, forever, with no log line. The websocket handler that raises creator-dump alerts had the same. STOP did not reach a buy already queued behind an exit.

**Security.** The pre-sign guard had four bypasses, each of which drains the wallet with an ordinary-looking buy: `SystemProgram.assign` (reassigns the wallet's owner program) fell through an unchecked branch; top-level SPL `Transfer` was not blocked; `ComputeBudget` was allow-listed with its data never read, so an unbounded priority fee passed; and naming the attacker inside a router instruction whitelisted a full-balance transfer to them. Separately, CORS was applied to *every* route including the document carrying the API bearer token, so any page on any other local port could read `__SNIPER_API_TOKEN__` out of the HTML and drive every trading endpoint; `/api/server/shutdown` was exempt from the token entirely; and `originGuard` read only `Origin`, which a DNS-rebinding page omits.

**Resilience.** The WS endpoint ignored the rejected-key latch the HTTP endpoint honoured, so a 401'd key left every socket retrying a dead URL. The copy trader never rebuilt its connection on failover. RPC health was fed by two peripheral call sites and nothing on the trading path, so a 429 storm was invisible to the signal meant to notice it. `sendRawTransaction` pinned `maxRetries: 3` and nothing rebroadcast afterwards. No RPC call had a per-call timeout, so one dead socket could pin the trade queue forever.

## What is deliberately NOT fixed

- **The PumpPortal router allow-list.** `txIntentGuard` allow-lists an undocumented, upgradeable third-party program. Its CPI is invisible to the guard, so a compromised router upgrade cannot be caught by inspection — the new per-trade ceilings bound what one transaction can take, but they do not remove the trust. The way out is `localTxBuild`, which constructs the pump.fun instruction directly; enabling it costs several RPC calls per trade and is an **operator decision**, not a patch.
- **On-chain pool verification** (`playbookRouter`): the venue for a buy and every later sell is still taken from the payload, unverified. `KILL-CRITERIA` restart criterion #4 names this; it is a feature, not a defect fix.
- **The strategy itself.** The prior study's NO-EDGE conclusion is untouched. This work makes the bot safe and honestly measurable; it does not make it profitable.

## For the operator

Two behaviours change on day one and you should know about both:

1. **Spending is now bounded.** The shipped ceilings are 1 SOL/hour, 2 SOL/session, 0.25 SOL per mint, 60 buys/hour, 3 concurrent, a 0.01 SOL wallet floor, a 0.5 SOL session loss cap, and a halt after 5 consecutive failed buys. If any of those is too tight for your wallet, **raise it** — `POST /api/governor/limits`, and every refusal names the ceiling that bound and its value. `GET /api/governor` shows where you stand. The limits and the halt persist across restarts on purpose: a restart is the natural reaction to a runaway, and it must not be the thing that clears the breaker that stopped it.

2. **All-in sizing is off.** If you were relying on it, it is a feature flag you turn on deliberately; it will no longer switch itself on when you save a setting.

If you still have the machine that ran the incident, collect `bot.log`, `.copy-trader.json` and `.open-positions.json` from the install directory before doing anything else. The `[Flags] Enabled:` line in the log says which safety flags were live, which is the single most useful fact for confirming this diagnosis against your session.
